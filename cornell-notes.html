Summary (refined)

The first chapter is an introduction to what the book is about. Chris drew differences between his negotiation techniques and what was developed academically: the academic approach assumes people are rational, but he says we are irrational, emotionally driven animals. He also leveraged the 2 systems to make negotiation work, which is by understanding System 1 to influence System 2, through how you frame and deliver your questions and statements. He also put negotiation in a wider context of application, which is not just in high-stakes situations but everyday life, where negotiation is basically communication with results and purpose: you are trying to get something from or with someone. It serves two functions: information gathering and behavior influencing. The FBI's approach put emotions and emotional intelligence at the center. The centerpiece is Tactical Empathy: negotiation relies on active listening, a powerful tool, because people want to be understood and listening is the cheapest, most effective concession. Combined with an understanding of emotions and psychology, it influences the decision of the counterpart.

1 · ★ Key ideaHypotheseshold several, then test
🔍 Detailsdiscovery comes first

1. Assumptions blind, hypotheses guide

Great negotiators reveal the surprises they are certain exist. Hold several hypotheses and use new information to test and winnow them.

The early goal is a mindset of discovery: extract and observe as much as you can.

Interestingly this aligns with the design thinking method: Discovery (list out hypotheses) -> Empathise -> Ideate -> Validate. Here he's describing the first step, and the next step is about Empathise (tactical empathy). This is real alignment with my work as a PM.

2 · ★ Key ideaIntellectually agilethe situation keeps changing
🔍 Detailsemotionally open too

2. A fluid situation

Assumptions show an unchanging, often flawed version of the situation. New facts do not get in.

Question the ones others accept on faith. You stay emotionally open, and intellectually agile to a fluid situation.

Do not commit to assumptions. View them as hypotheses and test them rigorously.

3 · ★ Key ideaPronounssmall self, big role
🔍 DetailsPinocchio: the lie grows

3. Watch we / they and I

The less important he makes himself, the more important he probably is. Watts talked up the “others.” He was the ringleader.

Lots of “I,” “me,” and “my” usually means the real power to decide lies elsewhere.

Liars use more words and more third-person pronouns, to put distance between themselves and the lie. The number of words grew along with the lie.

4 · ★ Key ideaWhy listening is hardthe mind is already full

4. Three reasons you miss it

Selective listening: a bias for consistency rather than truth. You hear what you want.

About seven pieces of information at once. Past that, you are overwhelmed.

A state of schizophrenia: each person is listening to the voice in their own head. Two people, four voices.

5 · ★ Key ideaActive listeningone focus, both voices quiet
🔍 Detailsthey feel safe

5. One focus

Instead of your argument, make the other person and what they say your sole and all-encompassing focus.

That quiets the voice in your head and in theirs. They feel safe, and a real conversation can start.

The goal of active listening is Discovery. Active listening should be the first step.

6 · ★ Key ideaWants and needswants are the door
🔍 Detailsneeds stay hidden

6. Talk about wants, find the needs

Wants are easy to say: the aspiration of getting our way, and an illusion of control.

Needs imply survival, the minimum that makes us act, so they make us vulnerable.

Talking about wants is how you discover needs. You do not start with either. You start by listening.

7 · ★ Key ideaSlow it downhurry feels like not being heard

7. Slow. It. Down.

Going too fast is a mistake negotiators are prone to. People feel they are not being heard, and trust slips.

Slow the process down and you calm it down. If someone is talking, they are not shooting.

This has implications in everyday conversation as well. Going too fast in conversation oftentimes doesn't serve one well.

Summary

The most key point I learned is about being aware of assumptions. Instead of making assumptions and defaulting them to fact and acting accordingly, we should put them as hypotheses, rigorously test them against new information that we receive. By doing that, we can be intellectually agile and respond well in a fluid situation. This also means our most important goal at the outset is to observe and collect as much info as we can. And to do that, it requires active listening. Listening is actually hard due to 3 reasons: we often listen selectively due to our cognitive bias for consistency, we get overwhelmed easily only holding about 7 pieces of info at the same time, we have multiple voices in our head that make it hard for us to listen to what's going on. The goal of listening is to identify what our counterpart needs and get them to talk to shed light on those needs. There are differences between needs and wants. Where wants are aspirations, easy to talk about, needs are more survival and run behind the scenes to motivate one's actions. And needs make one more vulnerable. Last point, though not directly related to the content so far, it's about the pace of conversation. Going too fast often erodes trust, makes the other person feel like they are not being heard.

1 · ★ Key ideaThe voicedelivery flips the switch
🔍 Detailswarmth comes back as a reflex

1. How you are

People focus on what to say or do. How we are is easier to enact, and it is the most immediately effective influence.

On a mostly unconscious level, the other person grasps what you feel. Not by thinking it through.

“your most powerful tool in any verbal communication is your voice.” It can flip distrust to trust, and nervous to calm.

2 · ★ Key ideaThree tonesplayful is the default
🔍 Detailsdownward means you've got it covered

2. Late-night DJ, playful, assertive

The late-night FM DJ voice is the voice of calm and reason. Downward inflection says you are in control. Upward inflection sounds like a question and invites them to lead.

  • DJ voice: use it selectively, calm and slow, so you sound trustworthy without triggering defensiveness.
  • Positive/playful: the default. Relax and smile while you talk.
  • Assertive: rare. It signals dominance, and they push back.

Late night DJ voice: safe, soft, slow, reassuring. 2 ways of using Late Night DJ voice. Inflecting downward shows you're in control, you've got it covered. Inflecting upward opens a door as you bring in some uncertainty, inviting the other person to take the lead.

3 · ★ Key ideaMirroringinsinuate similarity
? Guiding QWhat is mirroring?

3. Why a mirror works

Mirroring, also called isopraxism, is imitation. We copy each other to comfort each other. Speech, body, words, tempo, tone. Usually unconscious, and a sign of rapport.

Done on purpose, it is the art of insinuating similarity. “We fear what’s different and are drawn to what’s similar.”

Mirroring is imitation. It's the art of insinuating similarity.

What is the biological principle behind mirroring? We fear what's different and are drawn to what's familiar. Birds of a feather flock together. Mirroring talks to the unconscious that "You & me — we are alike."

4 · ★ Key ideaLast three wordsthen four seconds of quiet
🔍 Detailstips rose 70 percent

4. How to mirror

For a negotiator the mirror is words only. Repeat the last three words, or the critical one to three. Then be quiet for at least four seconds, so the mirror can work.

They will reword what they said. They will not say it the same way twice. That keeps them talking, buys you time, and shows their strategy.

Waiters who repeated the order back earned tips 70 percent higher than waiters who praised the order.

Disagree without being disagreeable: DJ voice, “I’m sorry,” mirror, silence, repeat.

Things that we can mirror in each other.

Mirroring is more effective in creating connections than positive reinforcement.

The keys to deal with Pitbull Type A.

5 · ★ Key ideaMirror, don't quizrespect, then clarity

5. The same ask, a different scene

The intention behind most mirrors is “Please, help me understand.”

“What do you mean by that?” is likely to incite irritation. A mirror gets the clarity and signals respect.

An example of how mirroring works much better in expressing the same idea. Mirroring creates agreeableness / enables collaboration settings before the content is delivered. That's how it can help us be disagreeable in an agreeable way, along with the late-night DJ voice and the use of silence. On the other hand, a question like "What do you mean by that?" though in meaning shows no irritation or defensiveness, is likely to set up the scene that way. The way of delivery sets an environment for the content to come across successfully, in a way that the other is more likely to cooperate.

Next: Chapter 3 — Don’t Feel Their Pain, Label It. Carry over: the session goal still needs one real conversation where you will try mirroring. Labeling is the next chapter, so the two principles in the summary get their rules there. Keep the voice, the last three words, and the four seconds of silence with the psychology under each.

Summary

Mirroring is a technique to insinuate similarity and enable a setting for collaboration — it's backed by the principle "Birds of a feather flock together." We are attracted to what feels familiar. Silence is a technique to pace yourself and ensure the stability of the conversation, create space for the other to talk — it's backed by the fact that when we go too fast, the other may not feel heard and trust is eroded. Labeling is a technique of empathy where the other's feeling is vocalized for recognition. It's backed by 2 psychological principles: (1) when their emotions are named, the other feels acknowledged and that what they feel is validated; (2) by labeling, it quiets the emotions and enables collaboration.

1 · ★ Key ideaEmotion as a toolnot an obstacle

1. From obstacle to means

Old advice: poker face, don’t get emotional. Once people get upset, rational thinking goes out the window.

Good negotiators identify and influence emotions, their own first. For them, emotion is a tool.

In this part, the author reframed emotions from obstacle to tool. He first explained how emotions derail communication by disabling one from making sensible decisions. And by precisely labeling emotions of oneself and the other, that can release the person from the control of that specific emotion. Precision is important to take away emotion's power.

3. Hear what is behind the feeling

Tactical empathy is understanding feelings and mindset in the moment, and hearing what is behind those feelings, so you gain influence in the moments that follow.

You can hold their point of view while you talk. The interviewer’s question is the feeling to hear behind; label that, then answer.

5 · ★ Key ideaLabelingspot, name, repeat back

5. How labeling is done

Not only step into their shoes. Spot the feelings, turn them into words, then calmly and respectfully repeat them back.

Naming a fear applies rational words to it and disrupts its raw intensity.

How empathy is done step by step. And to do it, it requires one to step into the other's shoes.

6 · ★ Key ideaSilence after the labeldon’t expand

Say It seems like, It sounds like, It looks like. Do not start with “I.”

Once you’ve thrown out a label, be quiet and listen. Expanding with a question kills the reveal.

Important rule: after labeling / mirroring, be silent. DON'T expand on what we've said. We must leave it to let the person reveal themselves.

1 · ★ Key ideaUnderlying, not presentingnegatives diffuse, positives reinforce

1. What a label is for

Emotions have two levels: the presenting behavior you can see, and the underlying feeling that motivates it.

Label the underlying one. Negatives diffuse. Positives reinforce.

Labeling is not about the surface presenting part, but about labeling the underlying feeling. That's why it's essential to put yourself in the other's shoes to feel what they are feeling.

Labeling is not just for diffusing negative feelings but also to reinforce positive ones.

2 · ★ Key ideaGo at the negativefearless, deferential

2. Do not sugar-coat

Go directly at negative dynamics. If it is your mistake, acknowledge their anger. “Look, I’m an asshole” is Voss’s version.

“I don’t want this to sound harsh …” denies the negative and gives it credence.

The more negative the emotions towards you, the more urgent it is to go direct and label it. If it's your mistake, acknowledge their anger by using yourself: "Look I'm an asshole" "Bless me father, I have sins."

3 · ★ Key ideaObserve firstno reaction, no judgment
? Guiding Qtheir negativity, or yours first?

3. Observe, then label, then replace

Deal with negativity by observing it without reaction and without judgment. Then label. Then replace with compassionate, solution-based thoughts.

Stressing on observe, WITHOUT reaction / judgment. Then label and replace.

4 · ★ Key ideaPresenting vs underlyingjail, not “let you go”
? Guiding Qhow are the two Harlem labels different?

4. Name the fear that drives it

“Let you go” is the presenting want. Everyone already knows it. “You don’t want to go back to jail” is the underlying fear.

Same split: cranky grandfather / lonely. Donor’s money fear / Girl Scout memories. The second label got “You understand me” and “I trust.”

Not just label the sadness; they describe the situation / acknowledge what's been going on. This seems to help set the same context for both, let the other see that you also see what they see.

5 · ★ Key ideaAccusation auditsay the worst first

5. List every terrible thing they could say

Write every charge they could make about you. Say those first. Said aloud they often sound exaggerated, so the other person talks you down.

This is similar to the labeling when you made a mistake. Accusation audit to acknowledge the other's feeling. This is different from our tendency to sugar-coat the talk… We must do the opposite: ADMIT the accusation and it DIFFUSES.

→ Try: one real conversation. List the worst they could say. Say it first.

Summary (refined)

There are 2 layers of emotions: presenting behavior and underlying emotions that motivate it. Presenting feelings are what is shown to others, underlying feelings are what motivate the presenting, and they are what we need to label. “Let you go” is presenting; “you don’t want to go back to jail” is the underlying fear. Labeling is to acknowledge the emotions underneath. Labeling negative feelings diffuses them and labeling positive feelings reinforces them. Once the primary emotion is acknowledged, the shift can be sudden: “You understand me.” “I trust you’ll find the right project.” Labeling fears interrupts the amygdala and generates safety, well-being, and trust. Accusation audit works by acknowledging all the negative feelings the other has towards you to diffuse them and get the other to feel understood, which enables effective conversation afterwards. Avoid saying something like I don't want to be rude… This is denying, and it backfires / reinforces the negative. Admitting the accusations will do the opposite. The sequence of effective labeling: observe without reaction / judgment, label, silence, replace the negative with positive, compassionate, and solution-based thoughts. Know that though these are powerful tools, it takes time to get people to the right state of mind.

1 · ★ Key ideaNo starts itnot the end, a pause
🔍 Detailsstatus quo, then they can listen

1. “No” is the start

“No” is the start of the negotiation, not the end of it. It is often a temporary decision to keep the status quo. Change is scary, and “No” buys a little safety.

Once they have protected themselves, they can relax and hear you. Amy’s “No’s” were the gateway to the one “Yes” that mattered.

Think of experiment. Each failure helps you understand the subject matter better and adjust to get to the goal. This is how NO helps while YES is a dead end.

2 · ★ Key ideaSafe and in controltwo primal urges
🔍 Detailsretrain how you hear No

2. Autonomy first

Everyone is driven by two primal urges: the need to feel safe and secure, and the need to feel in control.

Give them permission to say “No.” The emotions calm. They can actually look at the proposal.

To work with NO well, we need to retrain ourselves in hearing NO and interpret it as something more specific, rather than default it to rejection.

3 · ★ Key ideaThree yesesthey sound the same

3. Counterfeit, confirmation, commitment

A counterfeit “yes” hides a no, or buys time. A confirmation “yes” is a reflex with no promise of action. A commitment “yes” leads to action.

The Yes-pattern scripts got yes after yes, then a final no. Yes may not mean positivity or agreement; it can be a counterfeit that hides real motives, for example avoidance.

4 · ★ Key ideaInvite a Nothey feel in the driver’s seat
🔍 DetailsIs now a bad time?

4. Ask so the answer is No

Good negotiators welcome a solid “No” to start. It means they are engaged and thinking.

Don’t start with “Do you have a few minutes to talk?” Ask, “Is now a bad time to talk?”

Marti: “Do you want the FBI to be embarrassed?” then “What do you want me to do?” A strongly worded No, then a calibrated question, gives them the illusion of control while they solve the problem for you.

5 · ★ Key ideaForce a Noif they are not listening
🔍 Detailsmislabel, or ask what they don’t want

5. Two ways to get the word out

Feeling they can say No is not the same as getting them to say it. If they are not listening, antagonize them into No.

  • Mislabel an emotion or desire you know is wrong, so they correct you.
  • Make the topic what they don’t want, so they can freely say No.

It's not always easy to get people to say NO. One way is to mislabel their feelings on purpose, making it sound dramatic and exaggerated.

6 · ★ Key ideaAnti-nicenessauthentic, not plastic

6. Negotiate in their world

These tools go against “Be nice.” Plastic niceness blocks real connection. Triggering No is the anti-niceness ruse: not unkind, authentic.

A good negotiator’s power is less about him and more about what he can get from the counterpart: their situation, needs, and desires.

Persuasion is not about how bright or smooth or forceful you are. It’s the other party convincing themselves. Ask questions that open paths to your goals. It’s not about you.

Interestingly, when you say NO, you are more likely to be authentic with your feelings than YES. → ANTI-NICENESS

Summary (refined)

The chapter focuses on discussing the misunderstanding around YES and NO. He pivots to the idea that NO is the way to influence while YES can be just another dead end. NO is the start of the negotiation, not the end. It is often a temporary decision to keep the status quo. There are 3 kinds of YES: counterfeit, confirmation, commitment. What we aim for is commitment, where people take action. How NO helps: it creates safety and puts people in control. This dismisses their fear, reinforces that their basic needs are covered, and makes way for them to make decisions and collaborate. How to get people to say NO: (1) mislabel their emotions / motives on purpose (2) set the topic about what they don't want so they are free to say NO. Another way to open is to invite a NO: don't start with "Do you have a few minutes to talk?" Ask, "Is now a bad time to talk?" Another point about YES and NO: our natural tendency to say YES is to play nice. This is a surface-level understanding of YES and NO. What we need to change in our mindset is to see the real meaning and hidden motives behind YES and NO, instead of seeing them simply as agreement / alignment and disagreement / objection. A good negotiator does not put on a performance. They guide the counterpart to discover their goal as his own. Persuasion is not about how bright or forceful you are. It's the other party convincing themselves that the solution you want is their own idea.
